The bettor

The engine proves (⊢); the solver entails (⊨); both wait for certainty. The bettor makes a call. Under a uniform prior over deals, the probability of an envelope triple is just the fraction of surviving worlds that contain it — the worlds page, read as a distribution. The risk of waiting is the chance some rival is already certain — measurable over the same worlds, because inside each world every rival's hand (and everything they were shown) is fixed. Bet when confidence clears 0.9, or when the race forces your hand: when the chance a rival has it beats your own chance of being wrong.

Does it pay? The silent-run validation

What the bettor assumes

  • Uniform prior. Every deal consistent with the transcript is equally likely. True of the shuffle; it ignores what players' choices leak (a player rarely suggests cards she holds) — the posterior here is counting, not psychology.
  • Perfect-logician rivals, measured semantically. A rival counts as certain when every surviving world consistent with their view (their exact hand, plus the cards shown to them) agrees on the envelope. That is an upper bound on any real rival — and a tight one: on every six-player seed probed, the engine's envelope timing equalled this optimum.
  • Canonical shown-card resolution. Inside a hypothetical world, when a shower holds more than one matching card, the bettor assumes the lowest-numbered one was shown. The simulation actually picks uniformly; any deterministic pick is one sample from that coin.
  • It sleeps until the space is countable. No posterior is offered while the world count exceeds the cap — no pretence of precision over an unenumerated space.
